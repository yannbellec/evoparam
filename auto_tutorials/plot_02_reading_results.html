02: Reading the results¶

Get the numbers out of a fit, one by one or all in a table.

Introduction¶

In the first tutorial, report did everything at once: fit, summary and figure.

Here we look at what a fit gives back, and how to use it: read one number, check that the fit is good, keep all the numbers in a table, and choose the part of the curve to describe.

# Import what we need
from evoparam import fit_response, gen_response

Fitting without drawing¶

fit_response does only the fit: no figure, no summary. It is the one to use when you have many curves, or when you only want the numbers.

We use the same curve as in the first tutorial: a sharp bump at 25 ms and a broad one at 150 ms.

# Make the example curve
times, signal = gen_response([[25, -150, 10], [150, -80, 80]], seed=3)

# Fit the model, without drawing
fit = fit_response(times, signal, preset="ccep")

fit now holds everything evoparam found. print(fit) still shows the summary at any time.

print(fit)

Reading the bumps¶

The bumps are in fit.components, a list in order of time. Each bump has the three numbers of the first tutorial:

  • latency: when the bump peaks;

  • amplitude: its height above the background, in µV;

  • fwhm: its width at half its height (“full width at half maximum”).

Note

Inside evoparam, times are in seconds: a latency of 0.025 means 25 ms. Multiply by 1000 to read them in milliseconds.

# Go through the bumps, one line each
for bump in fit.components:
    print(bump.label, round(bump.latency * 1000, 1), "ms,", round(bump.amplitude, 1), "µV")

N1 25.0 ms, -152.1 µV
N2 151.9 ms, -77.4 µV

To keep one number, take the bump by its place in the list: [0] is the first one.

# The first bump, and its latency in ms
n1 = fit.components[0]
n1_latency_ms = n1.latency * 1000
print(round(n1_latency_ms, 1))

25.0

Each bump also has a few more numbers, all measured on the bump alone:

  • onset and offset: when the bump starts and ends (seconds);

  • area: the surface of the bump, the coloured area of the first tutorial (µV × seconds);

  • rise_time and decay_time: how long it takes to reach its peak, and to come back.

# When the first bump starts and ends, in ms
print(round(n1.onset * 1000, 1), round(n1.offset * 1000, 1))

16.3 33.6

Reading the background¶

The background is in fit.background. Here it is a curve that fades away, and the number to read is its time constant: how long it takes to lose about two thirds of its height. The smaller it is, the faster it fades.

# The time constant of the background, in ms
print(round(fit.background["tau"] * 1000, 1))

35.4

Is the fit good?¶

Before using the numbers, check that the model really follows the curve. Two numbers in fit.gof (“goodness of fit”) say it:

  • r2: how much of the curve the model explains, from 0 (nothing) to 1 (everything);

  • rmse: the typical distance between the curve and the model, in µV. It should be close to the level of the noise.

print(round(fit.gof.r2, 2), round(fit.gof.rmse, 1))

0.94 7.1

Note

Here R² is about 0.94: the model explains 94 % of the curve, and what is left is the noise we put in. A low R² means the model missed something: look at the figure (report) before trusting the numbers.

All the numbers in a table¶

to_dataframe puts every number of every bump in a table, one row per bump. This table can be sorted, plotted or saved like any spreadsheet (it needs the pandas package, already there in most notebooks).

# One row per bump; show a few columns
table = fit.to_dataframe()
table[["label", "latency", "amplitude", "fwhm", "area"]]
label latency amplitude fwhm area
0 N1 0.024951 -152.057403 0.009520 -1.491724
1 N2 0.151916 -77.413645 0.079193 -6.317850


To keep it, save it as a file that any spreadsheet program opens:

table.to_csv("my_results.csv")

Choosing the part of the curve to describe¶

The preset decides which part of the curve is described: for "ccep", from 9 to 500 ms after the stimulus. To look at another part, give a window, in seconds.

Here we keep only the first 100 ms: the broad bump at 150 ms is left out, and only the sharp one is described.

# Describe only 9 to 100 ms after the stimulus
early = fit_response(times, signal, preset="ccep", window=(0.009, 0.1))
print(early)

==================================================================
                    evoparam - RESPONSE MODEL

                     fit window: 9 to 100 ms
          background: exponential, time constant 50.0 ms

                      1 component(s) found:
            label        latency     amplitude     width
            N1           24.9 ms    -152.4 µV     9.5 ms

           fit quality: R2 = 0.97, noise level = 7.4 µV
  (latency: time of the peak; width: full width at half maximum)
==================================================================

Note

The preset matters too: it holds the settings made for one kind of recording. "ccep" is for responses to electrical pulses in the brain; "erp" is for responses recorded on the scalp (EEG), "sep" for touch, "tep" for magnetic pulses (TMS), and there are a few more.

Conclusion¶

A fit gives back its bumps (fit.components), its background (fit.background) and how good it is (fit.gof), and to_dataframe puts everything in one table. With window and preset, you choose what is described.
